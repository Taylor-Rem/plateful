Sandy reads like chain territory — the big-box corridors along State Street
and the 10600 South interchange are wall-to-wall national logos. But strip
the chains out, as [my July catalogue](/stories/the-online-ordering-gap)
does, and Sandy still holds 51 genuinely independent restaurants.

**26 of them — 51% — have no direct online-ordering channel.** In a suburb
where the default dinner move is ordering ahead from the car, half the
independent kitchens can't take that order except by phone.

## Busy restaurants, empty channel

Sandy's gap group is strikingly well-established: every one of the 26 has
more than a hundred Google reviews, and they average about **1,462 each** at
4.4 stars — second only to Orem's among the corridor's big cities. These
aren't hidden gems waiting to be discovered; they're already discovered.
What they're missing is the direct channel to convert all that earned
popularity into orders that don't pay
[marketplace rates](/stories/what-a-25-dollar-order-costs) — especially
when the chains they compete against all have apps and ordering baked in.

## The other half

Twenty-one Sandy independents run their own ordering, split evenly at the
top between [Toast and Olo with 6 each](/stories/what-utah-independents-actually-use),
and 4 more use DoorDash Storefront as their only channel —
[fine print applies](/stories/doordash-storefront). Against the chains'
in-house apps, Sandy's independents with first-party ordering at least
compete on even footing. The 26 without are competing against Chick-fil-A's
app with a phone number.

## The suburban math

A Sandy independent doing $4,000 a month through the apps at 25% pays
$12,000 a year — heavily weighted, in a residential suburb, toward
[repeat customers](/stories/your-regulars-are-the-expensive-part) who live
five minutes away. Across 26 such restaurants, the annual outflow is real
money leaving a city that prides itself on supporting local.
